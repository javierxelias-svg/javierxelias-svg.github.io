01 — About

Industrial Design taught me that form has to earn its function. I build the same way now, in software: autonomous multi-agent automations, a full business operating system, desktop apps, a knowledge base a model maintains as its own memory.

I build like it has to run without me watching: preflight checks, crash handling, a human tap before anything real happens. Things have broken, collapsed even, and every time I came back, found the bug, and fixed it. Two years as a butler at The St. Regis and two years at Optum (UnitedHealthcare) gave me the other half of the job, explaining technical things to people in the words they actually use.

Featured · running today

CareerOS — the system that found this job.

A job-search engine I designed and run: it reads 186 employer job boards directly from their own APIs, scores every listing against rules I wrote about pay, portability and the kind of work I can sustain, remembers what it has already shown me, and delivers a digest to Telegram each morning.

The non-obvious decision: it forbids the sentence “this should work.” Nothing is done because it looks done, only when it ran and left a receipt. Honesty is enforced by the machine, not by willpower.

Last run: 11,280 listings across 186 boards, 195 job pages read in full. I designed it, I run it, I debug it when it breaks, and I verify what it produces. I direct the code — I don't hand-write it.

How I debug it: three times it told me something confidently wrong →
The story · why a barrio
Intention
I'm building a life I don't need to escape from. CareerOS is how I look for the work that fits it: every employer's own job board, not just job sites, and every listing held to a pay bar and a portability bar I wrote down.
What happened
First commit July 6, 2026, and 128 commits since. It ran on my Mac, then moved to GitHub's servers in late September. It now reads about 185 boards and over 11,000 listings a run. The first application it led me to went out in October.
What I learned
A ranking system encodes a definition of "good," so write that definition down before you build it. And when I didn't act on its picks, the problem was the system, not me: it was inflating salaries 2.3×.
The symbol
A hillside barrio is built house by house by the people who live in it, and it's lived in every day. This is the system I live in: every block on that hill is one listing it read.

XPT OS

An outreach engine I built for my own search: a live-in caretaker arrangement in Mallorca, where the best ones are never posted. Every week a cloud routine searched over a dozen scattered sources (property agencies, buyer's agents, local connectors), scored each lead against a weighted rubric with hard vetoes, deduped against a permanent ledger so no one was contacted twice, and sent me drafts on Telegram to review and send myself.

The cloud sandbox blocked outbound network calls, so I used a git commit as the message queue to get work off the box.

Shipped · ran 17 times, June–September 2026 · 41 emails sent, 4 replies
The story · why a sugar mill
Intention
Free housing in the Mediterranean would turn everything I earn into savings, and the best caretaker arrangements are never posted; they come through people. So I built a machine to find those people.
What happened
June to September 2026: 17 weekly runs without a miss. I sent 41 emails between June 22 and July 22 and got 4 replies, one asking for my profile. I stopped sending in August; the machine kept running without me until late September.
What I learned
Job boards fill a small share of openings; most move through trust. And if a schedule matters, own the clock yourself and let the platform own only the work.
The symbol
Puerto Rico's sugar mills were built to send the island's work overseas. This one was built to send me overseas. The machinery never failed. I'm the one who stopped bringing it cane.

Web OS

A web-design business encoded as software: a five-stage system (source → design → outreach → CRM → close) that finds businesses with weak sites, produces $10K-quality builds, and runs the sale. ~2,750 lines of Python across 21 scripts plus a Next.js build starter.

The story · why rebar
Intention
Find businesses whose websites were hurting them, build them a site that looks like it cost $10,000, and close it at $2,500. Not a company; a few clients to pay the bills.
What happened
Email campaigns ran in June 2026; one hit 17% bounces and paused itself. In July I wiped version 1 and switched to cold calls and a $550 site. My first referred client, a small business in Vieques, got a free practice site. No paid deals yet.

Fig. 04 — Toldo azul · a blue tarp after Hurricane María
It fell, and its fall wrote my rules

ELiaS

A 9-agent personal AI operating system, built the way I'd want it if something real depended on it. ~9,700 lines: a shared resilience layer (preflight that exits clean instead of crash-looping, crash wrapper with alerting, Keychain secret fetch), a 4-provider LLM failover cascade, and a dead-man's switch that alerts from off the machine so a dead Mac still raises an alarm.

A hard rule: machine-written knowledge is quarantined from anything the system tells me about myself. Every real-world action needs a human tap.

9 agents ran unattended, May–June 2026 · the resilience rules I build by came from how it failed
The story · why a blue tarp
Intention
If I'm going to sell autonomous AI systems to businesses, I have to run one on myself first, unattended, for months, and find out what actually breaks.
What happened
Nine agents and about 9,700 lines ran on my Mac. On June 13, 2026, an auto-commit plugin removed all 60 files from the vault in ten minutes. I recovered the code from git; the system never ran again.
What I learned
Monitoring that lives inside the thing it monitors isn't monitoring: the dead-man's switch died with the man. Every rule I build by now, preflight checks, crash alerts, an alarm from off the machine, came from how this one fell.
The symbol
After Hurricane María, blue tarps covered the island's roofs while people rebuilt. A blue tarp means it fell and someone's still here. The rafters going up are the rules I took from the fall.

2nd Brain OS

A knowledge base a language model maintains as its own memory: an Obsidian vault where Claude Code is librarian and writer, with a three-tier context budget and a hand-written task router that answers any question in a capped number of reads.

A ground-truth file the model is forbidden to exceed, and autonomous writes tagged unverified, so machine claims stay separable from human fact. 176 agent-authored pages, nightly backup.

Live · 176 pages written by agents, backed up nightly · scheduled layer retired
The story · why Arecibo
Intention
Give a language model a long-term memory it keeps up on its own, without letting it rewrite the truth, because a model with write access will confidently corrupt what it touches.
What happened
82 commits since May 2026 and 176 pages written by agents. The knowledge layer is live and growing. The scheduled agents have been down since mid-May, failing on missing script paths, and the vault says so.
What I learned
A system that documents its own automation has to verify that documentation, or the documentation becomes the most confident liar in the vault.
The symbol
The great dish at Arecibo still stands; the platform that hung above it fell in 2020. The knowledge is the dish. The agents were the platform.

03

How I made this island

I built this whole portfolio with Claude Code. Every block, building, person and sound on the island is code: no 3D model files. I directed every piece and judged it by eye, Claude Code wrote it, and nothing went in until it ran in a real browser, on a desktop and a phone.

2 days · 20 versions · ~1,850 lines · 0 model files

It started as Greek temples. The first version put an acropolis on the island, one temple per project. I scrapped it for Puerto Rico itself: a barrio, a colmado, a sugar mill, a blue tarp.

What broke on the way, and how it got fixed

  • Everyone had one leg. A box drawn from right to left came out empty, so every person, drummer and vejigante lost the left leg. It showed on Benito first.
  • Parrots bigger than houses. The first cotorras had wings wider than a building, and the plastic chairs stood 1.6 m tall. People and props were rebuilt at true scale: a person is about 1.75 m beside a 2 m door.
  • Coquíes that never got louder at night. The line that turned them up was sitting inside a comment, so it never ran.
  • Rain that wasn't there. The shower over El Yunque switched itself off 40% of the time, and vanished completely with Reduce Motion on.
  • Taps that did nothing on a phone. Lifting a finger counts as "leaving" the screen, and that hid each explanation the instant it appeared. Caught by tapping an emulated iPhone with real touch events.
  • Puerto Rico sideways. On phones the final view showed the island turned on its side. Now it ends north-up, like a map.
  • Silence on iPhone. The silent switch muted the coquíes until the page asked to play as media, like a video.
  • An engine that broke while looking fine. A stray line broke the code twice while the browser kept serving the old copy. Now every change is syntax-checked and versioned before it loads.
  • A project card that told the wrong story. XPT OS said 18 drafts and 1 email. Its own records said 41 sent and 4 replies. Every claim on this page was checked against its source files.
  • Music we weren't allowed to use. The bomba recordings I loved most were all rights reserved. The plena you hear is one whose owner allows reuse, credited at the bottom.

That's how I build: make it, break it on purpose, fix what it shows me, and keep the receipt.
